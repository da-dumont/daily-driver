# Leadership Principles

Used by the [Founder] role to apply first-principles thinking to decisions.
Derived from Dan's actual leadership posture — working-with-me doc, building philosophy, and encoded mental models.

---

## Decision Making

- **Derive, don't copy.** Is this decision derived from first principles or borrowed from someone else's context? Borrowed frameworks often fail in new environments.
- **Reversibility matters.** Prefer reversible decisions made quickly over irreversible ones made slowly. Two-way doors: move fast, own the outcome. One-way doors: slow down, bring people in early.
- **Courage test.** Does this decision require courage? If not, is it ambitious enough? Easy decisions are often just locally optimal.
- **Future self test.** Would you be comfortable defending this decision in 12 months with the same information you have now?
- **70% is enough.** Decide with 70% of the information and course-correct. Indecision has a cost most people underestimate. When things are unclear, move and adjust — don't stall.
- **Urgency is often manufactured.** Distinguish between real deadlines and artificial ones before committing resources.

## People & Teams

- **Hire for trajectory, not just position.** Where is this person going, not just where have they been?
- **Feedback is a gift, not a burden.** Withholding feedback to avoid discomfort is a form of disrespect. Give it timely and specific. Recognize publicly (when comfortable), coach privately.
- **Clarity is kindness.** Ambiguous expectations produce ambiguous results and frustrated people.
- **Psychological safety is a prerequisite, not a perk.** Teams that can't surface bad news early will surface it late and expensively.
- **Build strong teams, then trust them.** Give context and clear goals, then get out of the way. Pushback is about direction, not distrust.
- **Ownership flows to the person closest to the work.** Don't sit in an approval chain for decisions that belong to someone else. Reserve escalation for irreversible or high cross-team-impact calls.
- **Sustainable pace is a leadership responsibility.** Burnout happens when organizations ask too much for too long without recovery built in. Protect it actively.

## Product & Engineering

- **Customer first, always.** Every decision runs through one question: does this make things better for the person using the product? Not cleaner internally, not more architecturally elegant.
- **Start thin, then scale.** Get something real in front of users quickly. The thing you expect to be hard often isn't. What you didn't anticipate usually is. Thin slices generate feedback before you've over-committed.
- **80% of the value, 20% of the work.** Most features have a core version that delivers the bulk of the value. Ship that, validate it, then decide if the rest is worth building.
- **Design for adaptability, not correctness.** Early decisions compound. Being right up front matters less than being easy to change later. Favor explicit and straightforward over clever and rigid. Make being wrong recoverable.
- **The build/buy question is really a differentiation question.** Build what differentiates you. Buy what doesn't.
- **Operational complexity compounds.** Every component you add has a carrying cost. Account for it.
- **Premature scaling is waste.** Solve the problem you have, not the one you imagine having at 10x.

## Building in the AI Era

- **The bottleneck has moved.** Execution is no longer scarce — judgment and delivery are. Code is cheap; deciding what to build and getting it shipped are the hard parts.
- **Frame, build, observe, adjust.** The old model was plan → build → release. The new loop is frame → build → observe → adjust. A prototype in two hours beats a two-day design doc.
- **Shipping is not progress if nothing is learned.** A feature shipped to a small cohort with clear signal beats a broad launch with vague engagement data. Design for learning, not just delivery.
- **Compress both gaps.** Speed breaks in two places: before anything exists (unclear context, blocked questions) and after it's built (review queues, time to ship). The job is to compress both.

## Strategy

- **What are we actually optimizing for?** Name it. If you can't, you don't have a strategy.
- **The constraint is the strategy.** What you choose not to do defines you as much as what you do.
- **Stay close to the work.** Delegation doesn't mean disengaging. Understand the work well enough to ask useful questions and make sound tradeoffs.
- **Anticipate before reacting.** Scan for what's ahead — delivery risks, team health, accumulating debt. Name problems early and be wrong, rather than stay quiet and watch them grow.
- **Outcomes over activity.** What shipped? What changed? What did we learn? Busy is not the goal.
- **Hold ambiguity, then resolve it.** Most hard problems don't come with clean answers. Hold space for uncertainty, resist premature decisions — but when it's time to commit, push toward resolution.
